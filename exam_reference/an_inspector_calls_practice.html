Core Essay Plans

Use these as a framework to structure your 45-minute essays. Each plan follows: introduction, three points with embedded quotes, conclusion.

Theme 1 — Social Responsibility (Capitalism vs. Socialism)
Priestley wrote the play in 1945 but set it in 1912 to highlight the disastrous consequences of capitalist selfishness. He uses the Inspector as a mouthpiece for socialism and social responsibility.
Point 1
Mr Birling's capitalist ignorance. Birling rejects community ("as if we were all mixed up together like bees in a hive"). He represents the selfish, profit-driven mindset. Quote: "A man has to make his own way."
Point 2
The Inspector's socialist message. The Inspector acts as a catalyst for truth. His final speech is a direct plea to the audience. Quote: "We are members of one body."
Point 3
The younger generation's acceptance. Sheila and Eric accept their guilt, showing Priestley's hope that post-war Britain can change and embrace the welfare state. Quote: "The point is, you don't seem to have learnt anything." (Sheila)
Conclusion
Priestley warns that ignoring social responsibility will lead to disaster — "fire and blood and anguish" — a direct reference to the two World Wars.
Theme 2 — Generational Differences
Priestley divides the characters by age to show that the older generation is entrenched in out-of-date, prejudiced views, while the younger generation is capable of change and redemption.
Point 1
The rigid older generation. Mr and Mrs Birling are unchanging. Even after the Inspector is revealed as potentially fake, they immediately revert to their arrogant ways. Quote: "You're just the kind of son-in-law I always wanted." (Birling, ignoring Gerald's affair)
Point 2
The malleable younger generation. Sheila and Eric are deeply affected by Eva's death and become the moral centre of the play. Quote: "But these girls aren't cheap labour — they're people." (Sheila)
Point 3
Gerald Croft — the bridge. Gerald is young but holds older, traditional views, showing that upper-class attitudes are entrenched regardless of age. Quote: "Everything's all right now, Sheila. What about this ring?"
Conclusion
Priestley uses the youth to represent hope for a fairer, more compassionate future — but makes clear it is conditional on actually choosing differently.
Theme 3 — Social Class and Inequality
The comfortable Birling dining room sharply contrasts with the invisible suffering of Eva Smith. Priestley critiques the rigid class system of Edwardian England.
Point 1
The exploitation of the working class. Eva Smith is disposable to the Birlings. Arthur sacks her for wanting a few extra shillings; Sybil refuses her charity out of class prejudice. Quote: "Girls of that class..." (Sybil)
Point 2
Class and morality. The Birlings believe wealth makes them respectable. The Inspector flips this — showing the "respectable" citizens are hypocrites whose actions destroyed a human life.
Point 3
The role of Edna. Edna the maid is the only visible working-class character. Her minimal lines highlight how the lower classes were silenced. Her announcement of the Inspector marks the interruption of the capitalist bubble by reality.
Conclusion
Eva Smith is a universal symbol for the working class — "millions and millions of Eva Smiths." The play demands the dismantling of class prejudice.
Theme 4 — Gender / Women in 1912
Priestley explores how women in 1912 were treated as second-class citizens, controlled by men, and judged by a double standard — particularly working-class women like Eva.
Point 1
The vulnerability of working-class women. Eva is exploited by every class level — fired by Birling over money, fired by Milwards over jealousy, used by Gerald and Eric. She has no power or recourse.
Point 2
The double standard. Arthur forgives Gerald's affair but is outraged by Eric's behaviour. Women were expected to be pure; men were excused their "mistakes."
Point 3
Sheila's emancipation. Sheila begins as a passive "little girl" who lets others speak for her. By Act 3, she is independent, handing back the ring and standing up to her parents.
Conclusion
Priestley uses Eva's tragedy and Sheila's awakening to show the necessary progression toward women's rights — a progression already underway by 1945 when the play was written.
