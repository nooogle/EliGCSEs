# An Inspector Calls — Practice & Essay Plans

**Paper:** 2, Section A  
**Marks:** 30 (+ 4 for SPaG)  
**Suggested time:** 45 minutes  
**Format:** Closed book — no extracts provided. You will get a choice of two questions and must answer only **one**.

## AQA-Style Sample Questions

### Character Questions
1. How does Priestley use the character of **Arthur Birling** to explore ideas about social responsibility?
2. How does Priestley present **Sheila Birling** as a character who changes throughout the play?
3. How far is **Eric Birling** presented as a sympathetic character?
4. How does Priestley use **Inspector Goole** to communicate his own political and social views?
5. How does Priestley present the character of **Sybil Birling** and her attitudes to the working class?

### Theme Questions
6. How does Priestley explore the differences between the **older and younger generations** in *An Inspector Calls*?
7. How does Priestley explore the theme of **social class and inequality**?
8. How does Priestley present the importance of **social responsibility**?
9. How does Priestley explore the position of **women in 1912 society**?
10. Explore how Priestley uses **dramatic irony and tension** to engage the audience.

## Core Essay Plans

*Use these plans as a framework to structure your 45-minute essays.*

### Theme 1: Social Responsibility (Capitalism vs. Socialism)

**Introduction:** Priestley wrote the play in 1945 but set it in 1912 to highlight the disastrous consequences of capitalist selfishness. He uses the Inspector as a mouthpiece for socialism and social responsibility.
- **Point 1:** **Mr Birling’s Capitalist Ignorance.** Birling rejects community ("as if we were all mixed up together like bees in a hive"). He represents the selfish, profit-driven mindset. *Quote: "A man has to make his own way".*
- **Point 2:** **The Inspector’s Socialist Message.** The Inspector acts as a catalyst for truth. His final speech is a direct plea to the audience. *Quote: "We are members of one body."*
- **Point 3:** **The Younger Generation's Acceptance.** Sheila and Eric accept their guilt, showing Priestley's hope that post-war Britain can change and embrace the welfare state. *Quote: "The point is, you don't seem to have learnt anything." (Sheila)*
- **Conclusion:** Priestley warns that ignoring social responsibility will lead to disaster ("fire and blood and anguish" – a reference to the World Wars).

### Theme 2: Generational Differences

**Introduction:** Priestley divides the characters by age to show that the older generation is entrenched in out-of-date, prejudiced views, while the younger generation is capable of change and redemption.
- **Point 1:** **The Rigid Older Generation.** Mr and Mrs Birling are unchanging. Even after the fake inspector is revealed, they immediately revert to their arrogant ways. *Quote: "You're just the kind of son-in-law I always wanted" (Birling ignoring Gerald's affair).*
- **Point 2:** **The Malleable Younger Generation.** Sheila and Eric are initially complicit in the upper-class bubble but are deeply affected by Eva's death. They become the moral center of the play. *Quote: "But these girls aren't cheap labour - they're people." (Sheila)*
- **Point 3:** **Gerald Croft — The Bridge.** Gerald is young but holds the same capitalist mindset as Arthur Birling. He shows that upper-class arrogance isn't just about age, but about protecting wealth and status. *Quote: "Everything's all right now, Sheila. What about this ring?"*
- **Conclusion:** Priestley uses the youth to represent his hope for a fairer, more compassionate future after 1945.

### Theme 3: Social Class and Inequality

**Introduction:** The physical setting of the comfortable Birling dining room sharply contrasts with the invisible suffering of Eva Smith. Priestley critiques the rigid class system of Edwardian England.
- **Point 1:** **The Exploitation of the Working Class.** Eva Smith is disposable to the Birlings. Arthur sacks her for wanting a couple of extra shillings; Sybil refuses her charity because of prejudice. *Quote: "Girls of that class..." (Sybil).*
- **Point 2:** **Class and Morality.** The Birlings believe their wealth makes them respectable. The Inspector flips this, showing that the "respectable" citizens are actually criminals and hypocrites.
- **Point 3:** **The Role of Edna.** Edna the maid is the only visible working-class character. Her minimal lines highlight how the lower classes were silenced. Her announcement of the Inspector ("Please, Sir, an inspector's called") marks the interruption of the capitalist bubble by reality.
- **Conclusion:** Eva Smith is a universal symbol for the working class ("millions and millions of Eva Smiths"). The play demands the dismantling of Edwardian class prejudices.

### Theme 4: Gender / Women in 1912

**Introduction:** Priestley explores how women in 1912 were treated as second-class citizens, controlled by men, and judged by a double standard—particularly working-class women like Eva.
- **Point 1:** **The Vulnerability of Working-Class Women.** Eva is exploited by every class level. She is fired by Birling over money, fired by Milwards over Sheila's jealousy, and used for sex by Gerald and Eric. She has no power.
- **Point 2:** **The Double Standard.** Arthur easily forgives Gerald's affair with Daisy Renton ("you must understand that a lot of young men-") but is outraged by Eric’s behaviour. Women were expected to be pure, men were allowed 'mistakes'.
- **Point 3:** **Sheila's Emancipation.** Sheila begins as a passive, consumerist "little girl" who lets her parents and fiancé speak for her. By Act 3, she is independent, handing the ring back and standing up to her parents. 
- **Conclusion:** Priestley uses Eva’s tragedy and Sheila’s awakening to show the necessary progression toward women's rights and equality (which got a massive boost between 1912 and 1945).
